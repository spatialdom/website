# What is a mother title, and why should a buyer care?

**3 min read · Last reviewed 2026-10-11**

A mother title is the certificate covering a larger tract before smaller lots receive their own separate certificates. Buying a piece of that tract can be legitimate, but a sketch of the proposed lot is not the same as an approved subdivision plan or an individual title in your name.

## The title covers more land than the lot being sold

Suppose a registered 4,000-square-meter property is offered as four 1,000-square-meter lots. The seller may advertise Lot B using a brochure or site sketch, while the Registry of Deeds still has a single certificate covering the whole 4,000 square meters. That certificate is what people informally call the mother title.

The question is not only whether the seller owns the parent property. It is whether the specific portion offered to you is legally and technically identifiable, can be transferred as intended, and can ultimately receive its own certificate.

## Separate titles require more than drawing dividing lines

Under Section 50 of PD 1529, subdivision of registered land generally involves a duly approved plan and corresponding technical descriptions. Section 58 addresses a conveyance of only part of the land covered by a certificate: the Register of Deeds will not issue a transfer certificate for that portion until the subdivision plan and descriptions have been verified and approved under Section 50.

An unsegregated conveyance may be annotated on the existing title pending issuance of a separate certificate. That does not make the administrative, survey, tax and registration steps disappear. Depending on the project, additional subdivision-development rules and approvals may apply beyond the land-registration procedure.

## Ask for the records that connect the promised lot to the title

Request a recent certified true copy of the mother title and check the registered owner, existing annotations, and whether the title is still current. Ask for the approved subdivision plan and the technical description for the exact lot being sold. Compare lot identifiers across the plan, contract, tax records and site layout.

A promise that 'the individual title is being processed' needs a verifiable status, responsible party, expected deliverables and a written allocation of costs and delays. Ask whether the parcel has legal access, whether there are easements or restrictions, and whether all necessary owners have agreed to the transaction.

## The biggest risk is buying a promise that cannot be completed

A physical fence or a seller's highlighted map is not enough to establish which approved lot you will own. Problems may arise from unresolved inheritance, mortgages on the parent property, a plan that has not been approved, missing access, or a mismatch between the offered parcel and the eventual subdivision.

Before substantial payment, have a lawyer review the sale documents and conditions for issuance of an individual title. A licensed Geodetic Engineer can check whether the proposed lot corresponds to the survey records. Parcel Plotter can illustrate the described shape, but it cannot approve a subdivision or issue a title.
