# Household profiling systems for LGUs

**3 min read · Last reviewed 2026-09-26**

A household profiling system is useful only when the information can support real local decisions and remain current enough to trust. Collecting a large form once is easy; maintaining a responsible local information system is the harder part.

## Start with the decisions the LGU needs to make

Before choosing fields, ask what the data is meant to support. Does the LGU need to identify communities with weak access to services, plan health or social programs, understand migration, prepare barangay development plans, or follow up with specific households? Different questions require different levels of detail.

This matters because household profiling can easily turn into 'collect everything just in case.' That creates more work, more stale data, and more privacy risk. A good system can explain why each major field exists and who actually needs to use it.

## Be precise about what a household is

The Philippine Statistics Authority defines a household as a person living alone or a group of persons who sleep in the same housing unit and share arrangements for preparing and consuming food. A household is therefore not automatically the same as a family, an address, or a house.

That distinction becomes practical when several households share one structure, when relatives live at the same address but maintain separate food arrangements, or when household membership changes. If the unit of record is unclear, counts and follow-up activities become inconsistent.

## Design for change, not just for the baseline survey

Local household data changes constantly. People are born, die, move in, move out, marry, separate, form new households, change occupations, or transfer to another barangay. CPD's Registry of Barangay Inhabitants and Migrants (RBIM) was designed specifically to capture demographic and household characteristics together with internal migration.

That suggests a better way to think about profiling: not as a finished census spreadsheet but as a maintained registry. Records need dates, update status, responsible users, correction procedures, and a way to preserve important changes rather than simply overwriting yesterday's information.

## Location makes household data more useful—but also more sensitive

Knowing that a barangay has 300 households with a certain need is useful. Knowing where service gaps are concentrated can be even more useful for planning routes, facilities, disaster response, or targeted programs. Spatial context turns a list into a pattern that local planners can act on.

But household-level location can also make people easier to identify. Public dashboards rarely need names and exact household points. A responsible system separates operational access from aggregate planning views and exposes only the detail necessary for the task.

## Data quality is an organizational responsibility

Software cannot decide on its own which household is duplicated, whether a person has truly transferred residence, or which office is authorized to correct a record. Assign clear roles for collection, review, approval, correction, and reporting. Record when information was last checked and, where appropriate, how it was obtained.

CPD guidance on RBIM describes a process that includes data collection, encoding, validation, analysis, and use. The important lesson is that the database is only one part of the system. People, procedures, training, and accountability determine whether the data stays useful.

## Privacy cannot be an afterthought

Household databases can contain personal and sensitive personal information. The Data Privacy Act applies to government processing, and National Privacy Commission rules require government agencies, including LGUs, to establish safeguards appropriate to the risks. Recent NPC and DILG guidance has also reinforced privacy compliance across LGUs.

In practice, that means collecting only what serves a legitimate purpose, showing an appropriate privacy notice, controlling access by role, protecting stored and transmitted data, defining retention and correction processes, and documenting who is responsible for the system. If an LGU cannot explain why a field is needed or who should see it, that field deserves another look.
