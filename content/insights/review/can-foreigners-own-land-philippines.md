# Can foreigners own land in the Philippines?

**3 min read · Last reviewed 2026-10-18**

> **Review copy.** Edit this Markdown file directly if you want to change the article. When you are satisfied, open [Issue #36](https://github.com/spatialdom/website/issues/36) and change the label from `insight:review` to `insight:approved`.

As a general rule, a foreign national cannot buy and own private land in the Philippines. The Constitution provides limited exceptions, and ownership of a condominium unit is a different question from owning a residential lot. Before relying on an advertised workaround, check the actual legal structure.

## The constitutional starting point

Article XII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution generally limits transfers of private land to persons or entities qualified to acquire or hold land of the public domain, with an exception for hereditary succession. This means a foreign buyer ordinarily cannot purchase a residential lot in their own name, even if they have lived in the Philippines for years or are married to a Filipino citizen.

Using another person's name as a supposed nominee is not a reliable substitute for ownership. It can create serious legal and financial exposure for everyone involved.

## Inheritance and former natural-born Filipinos need separate analysis

The constitutional exception for hereditary succession is narrow and should not be confused with a negotiated purchase or a gift structured to resemble an inheritance. Whether a particular inheritance qualifies depends on the facts and applicable succession law.

Article XII, Section 8 separately allows a natural-born Filipino who lost Philippine citizenship to acquire private land subject to statutory limits. The applicable limits and purpose differ by category, so eligibility should be confirmed before signing. A dual citizen who has validly reacquired Philippine citizenship is in a different legal position from someone who remains solely a foreign national.

## A condominium may be possible, but the ownership structure matters

The Condominium Act allows separate ownership of a condominium unit with associated rights in common areas. Foreign ownership may be possible where the project's land and common areas are held through a qualifying condominium corporation and the foreign participation limit is respected. Where common areas are owned directly as co-owners, Section 5 imposes restrictions on transfers to foreigners, apart from hereditary succession.

Do not reduce the answer to 'foreigners can buy 40% of any condominium.' Ask the developer or Registry of Deeds for the master deed, condominium corporation documents and confirmation of foreign ownership capacity for that specific project.

## Leasing and building ownership are not the same as land ownership

A lease can provide lawful use of land without transferring ownership, subject to the lease terms and applicable statutes. Separate ownership of structures can also arise under some arrangements. Neither arrangement should be marketed as equivalent to owning the land itself.

If a property is offered through a corporation, have counsel verify beneficial ownership, nationality requirements and anti-circumvention rules. The law looks beyond a label on the deed.

## Before paying a reservation fee

Identify whether the offer is for a lot, condominium unit, leasehold or another interest. Confirm the buyer's citizenship status and the exact ownership documents. Obtain a current certified true copy of the relevant title and get a Philippine property lawyer to review the proposed structure.

Parcel Plotter can help interpret a lot's technical description, but it cannot assess nationality eligibility or create legal ownership rights.
